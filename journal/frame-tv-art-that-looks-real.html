Frame TV art that looks like a painting, not a screen

Every guide tells you to turn the brightness down. That is good advice, and it gets you about halfway. The other half is the picture itself — and almost nobody talks about that.

Here is the whole problem in one sentence: a painting reflects light, a screen emits it. Your eye knows the difference before you consciously notice anything. Turning the brightness down narrows the gap. Choosing the right kind of image closes most of what is left.

I paint in ink and prepare files for The Frame, so I spend a lot of time looking at what survives the translation to a glowing panel and what does not. This is what I have learned.

What gives the screen away

Large areas of pure white. This is the biggest one, and it is the one people get wrong most often. On paper, white is the absence of ink — it sits back. On a screen, white is the panel at full output, so it pushes forward and glows. A minimalist print with a wide white margin looks serene on paper and looks like a lit rectangle on a TV.

Saturated colour. Pigment on paper can only be so vivid. A screen has no such limit, so saturated images immediately read as digital. Anything that looks punchy on your phone will look artificial on the wall.

Fine detail everywhere. At two or three metres away, dense detail turns into visual noise and the eye starts reading pixels instead of brushwork. Detail needs somewhere quiet to sit next to.

Perfectly clean gradients. Real paint has grain, bloom, uneven edges where the wash dried. A flawless digital gradient is the single most machine-looking thing you can put on that panel.

This is the honest part: not every beautiful print belongs on a Frame TV. Much of my own work is black ink on a wide field of white paper — on a wall it breathes, on a screen that field glows and the illusion collapses. For The Frame I choose different pieces: misty mountains, fog over water, storm skies. Work that lives in the middle of the tonal range.

The technical minimum

  • 3840 × 2160 pixels, 16:9. Anything smaller gets stretched, and stretching is instantly visible on a 55-inch panel.
  • Fill the frame. No borders baked into the file — the TV's own bezel is your frame.
  • Skip the digital mat. A mat is a physical object that catches real light. Simulated, it flattens and looks fake.
  • Brightness down, warmth up. Lower than feels right at first. Give your eyes an evening to adjust before you judge.
  • Turn on the motion sensor. Art mode running in an empty room is the fastest way to a bright rectangle nobody is looking at.

A test that takes ten seconds

Upload the image, step back to where your sofa is, and glance at it sideways — the way you would notice something while walking through the room, not the way you study it. If your first impression is "screen", no settings will fix that file. If your first impression is "picture", you have the right one.

Where to start
